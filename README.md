The AI Learning Paradox: Productivity, Cognitive Dependency, and Education Policy in Bangladesh 
Generative AI has entered students' lives much faster than most education systems were prepared for. A student can now open ChatGPT, Gemini, DeepSeek, or another AI tool and ask for an explanation of a difficult concept, generate ideas for an assignment, summarize a chapter, correct English, solve a programming problem, or prepare for an examination within seconds. For many students, this is genuinely useful. AI can work like a private tutor that is available at any time, especially for students who may not have access to expensive coaching, strong academic support, or high-quality learning materials.
This opportunity can be particularly important in Bangladesh. Students come from very different educational, linguistic, technological, and financial backgrounds. A student who struggles with an English textbook can ask AI to explain the same concept in simpler English or Bengali. Someone learning programming can ask for an explanation of an error instead of spending hours searching for the solution. Students can brainstorm ideas, organize their work, practice questions, and receive immediate feedback. Used in this way, AI can make learning more accessible and productive.
But the same convenience creates a serious problem.
There is a major difference between using AI to support thinking and using AI instead of thinking. A student who asks AI to explain why an answer is wrong is still participating in the learning process. A student who asks AI to produce the complete answer, copies it, and moves on may finish the task faster but may learn very little from it.
This is where the idea of cognitive offloading becomes important. Students have always used tools to reduce mental effort. Calculators reduce the need for manual calculation, search engines reduce the need to memorize information, and spelling software reduces the need to remember every spelling rule. Generative AI goes much further because students can now offload not only simple tasks but also activities such as brainstorming, reasoning, outlining, evaluating arguments, coding, and writing complete answers.
The concern, therefore, is not simply whether students use AI. Asking "Do you use ChatGPT?" tells us very little. The more important questions are: What do students ask AI to do? At what stage of learning do they use it? What thinking do they still do themselves? Do they verify AI-generated information? Can they explain the same concept without AI afterwards? And what happens when AI is suddenly unavailable?
For example, two students may both say that they regularly use AI. The first student may study a chapter independently and then ask AI to quiz them, challenge their understanding, or explain something they could not understand. The second may immediately ask AI to summarize the chapter, prepare the assignment, and generate the answers. Their frequency of AI use could be identical, but their learning behaviour is completely different.
This creates an AI learning paradox. AI may increase short-term productivity while potentially weakening some of the cognitive processes needed for long-term learning. Students may complete assignments more quickly and produce better-looking work while becoming less confident when they have to brainstorm, reason, explain, or solve unfamiliar problems without AI assistance. They may also develop an illusion of competence, where understanding an AI-generated explanation feels similar to actually being able to reproduce or apply that knowledge independently.
At the same time, simply banning AI would ignore its educational value and would probably not reflect how students are already learning. The more practical challenge is to understand how AI can be integrated into education without allowing it to replace the thinking that education is supposed to develop.
This issue is especially relevant for Bangladesh because student use of generative AI is developing rapidly while institutional practices, assessment methods, teacher preparation, and AI-related guidance are still adapting. In many situations, AI use is treated mainly as an academic integrity problem: either students are allowed to use it informally or they are told that using it is cheating. This leaves little guidance about the space between those two extremes.
Our project therefore focuses on how students in Bangladesh are actually using AI for learning, rather than simply measuring whether they have adopted it. We want to understand when AI behaves like a tutor, when it becomes a productivity tool, and when it starts functioning like a crutch.
The project will examine issues such as productivity, cognitive offloading, academic dependency, brainstorming behavior, verification of AI answers, confidence without AI, Socratic use of AI, exam preparation, and students' attitudes toward institutional AI policies. It will also compare different educational contexts, including secondary-level students and university students from general, engineering, and medical backgrounds.
Beyond surveys, the project can experimentally examine whether AI-supported preparation actually changes students' ability to think and communicate independently. For example, one group may prepare a debate or difficult topic with AI assistance while another group prepares without AI. Both groups can then be asked to explain their arguments, respond to unexpected questions, defend their reasoning, and demonstrate how well they actually understand what they prepared. Measures of cognitive engagement can then help determine whether higher immediate productivity comes with any reduction in independent cognitive effort.
Ultimately, the goal is not to prove that AI is either "good" or "bad" for education. That would oversimplify the issue. The more useful question is:
What type of AI use helps students learn, and what type of AI use slowly replaces the learning process itself?
Answering that question could help Bangladesh move away from treating generative AI only as a cheating problem and toward developing a more realistic educational approach. Instead of asking whether students should use AI, schools and universities may need to decide how AI should be used, what students must still be able to do independently, how AI-supported work should be assessed, and how teachers can encourage productive rather than dependent AI use.
The long-term objective is therefore to contribute toward an educational model in which AI improves access and productivity without removing the struggle, questioning, reasoning, and independent problem-solving through which real learning develops.
